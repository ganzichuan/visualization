      Good visualization: Our World in Data, “Cumulative CO₂ emissions” https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/cumulative-co-emissions?country=ZAF~GBR~BRA~CAN 

        I see Our World in Data’s “Cumulative CO₂ emissions” chart as a good visualization because it is well suited to its purpose, audience, and medium. The chart shows cumulative carbon dioxide emissions from fossil fuels and industry since 1750. Because the purpose is to show change over time, a line chart is an appropriate choice. It allows viewers to follow long-term historical trajectories and compare countries or regions without needing to interpret a more unfamiliar visual form. This visualization is strong in terms of its substantive qualities. The Our World in Data chart clearly identifies what is being measured: cumulative CO₂ emissions, excluding land-use change, measured in tonnes. It also provides the data source, the Global Carbon Budget, and includes methodological notes about what is and is not included. This connects to the course concept of “provenance rhetoric”: citing data sources is not only good practice for reproducibility, but also signals transparency and trustworthiness to viewers. The visualization is also effective perceptually. A simple two-dimensional line chart reduces unnecessary cognitive load because it uses a familiar chart type, clear axes, and a clean layout. Viewers do not have to spend much effort figuring out how to read the chart; they can focus instead on interpreting the pattern. This is important because extraneous cognitive load—difficulty created by the presentation itself—is one of the elements that designers can control. The chart is not completely neutral, because no visualization is. It encourages viewers to think about historical responsibility for carbon emissions by emphasizing cumulative emissions rather than, for example, annual or per-capita emissions. However, this rhetorical choice is not dishonest. The chart clearly defines its measurement, source, and limitations. In this sense, it is a good example of a visualization that makes an argument while still remaining transparent and substantively responsible.

        Bad visualization: “Gun deaths in Florida” https://www.businessinsider.com/gun-deaths-in-florida-increased-with-stand-your-ground-2014-2

        I classify “Gun deaths in Florida” chart provided above as a bad visualization because its design makes the data easy to misinterpret. The chart shows gun deaths in Florida before and after the state passed its “Stand Your Ground” law in 2005. The major problem is that the y-axis is inverted: the number of deaths increases downward rather than upward. As a result, a quick glance may suggest that gun deaths declined after the law, when the data actually show an increase. This is a serious perceptual problem. The course material explains that visualizations depend on human cognition and perception. Viewers generally expect higher positions on a vertical axis to represent larger values. By reversing this convention, the chart creates unnecessary cognitive load and works against the viewer’s normal way of reading a graph. This is not merely a stylistic issue. Since the visual form can produce the opposite first impression from the numerical data, the chart weakens its own substantive honesty. The chart also fails in terms of effective purpose and audience. If the intended purpose is to help a general audience understand the relationship between the 2005 law and gun deaths over time, then the visualization should make that relationship easier to evaluate. Instead, the inverted axis makes the viewer work harder and increases the chance of misunderstanding. The course material notes that rare or unfamiliar chart conventions increase cognitive load, while familiar forms usually make interpretation easier. Here, the use of an inverted y-axis is not justified by the data or the topic. The chart also raises concerns about rhetorical framing. As the course material argues, visualizations are rhetorical objects because they make choices about how reality is represented. In this case, the graphic’s design choice is not just non-neutral; it is misleading. A better version would use a conventional y-axis, clearer labeling, and possibly population-adjusted rates rather than absolute numbers. It should also clarify that a time-series visualization can suggest correlation but cannot by itself prove causation.

    - How could this data visualization have been improved?

      For the "Gun deaths in Florida," this visualization could have been improved by using a conventional y-axis in which larger numbers appear higher on the chart. Since the original chart inverted the y-axis, it created unnecessary cognitive load and made the data easier to misread, as a similar example we discussed in the class. A general audience usually expects upward movement to indicate an increase, so the chart’s design worked against normal visual perception. To improve its perceptual quality, the visualization should use a standard two-dimensional line chart or bar chart, with clearly labeled axes, a visible zero or baseline where appropriate, and a title that accurately describes the data. It should also include a clear source note and explain whether the numbers represent total deaths or population-adjusted rates, as the instructor reminded us a few times in class. Finally, if the visualization is meant to suggest a relationship between Florida’s “Stand Your Ground” law and gun deaths, it should clarify that the chart shows a temporal pattern, not proof of causation. These changes would reduce extraneous cognitive load, improve transparency, and make the visualization more substantively honest.
